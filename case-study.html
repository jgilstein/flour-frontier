The Network Reality

Why an Atlanta warehouse keeps coming up

Most of this plan runs inside what we already operate: five mills and six warehouses. The Southeast is the exception. Cities such as Atlanta, Charlotte, Nashville, Memphis and more sit in the strongest region in the data with no warehouse close enough to serve them well. When product sits closer, the freight gap that keeps us from competing mostly disappears. Orlando already proved it, so well that it is now bumping against its own capacity. Better local service and freight economics from an Atlanta warehouse would also improve DSR accessibility across the broader Southeast, not just in Atlanta itself. It is not a matter of if, but rather when the Atlanta warehouse will become a reality.

Product Fit

Some opportunities need a better product match, not just a lower price

Product fit came up more than freight or price in several conversations. Ethnic bread and bakery applications raised questions about dough stability and whether an enzyme-based system performs better than a malt-based one. In donuts, buns, and related bakery items, shelf life and day-two quality matter as much as flour cost. That does not change the ranking, but it changes how realistic some opportunities are: cleaner-label, enzyme-based, and untreated options could help GMI win back business on larger accounts, particularly in the West and Mid-Atlantic, and a more competitive 10.7% to 11% protein tortilla flour matters in South-Central markets with strong Hispanic growth. A recurring structural gap is bulk: GMI is strongest in bagged flour, while competitors have broader bulk networks, so the near-term priority is protecting the bagged-flour base while building selective specialty lanes around it.
The Recommendation

Four moves built for the network we have

Rebuild Texas around the market
Houston & Dallas
The Sysco distributor-label business went to Ardent last fall and is not realistically recoverable in the near term. Dallas is still growing (14.5% share, +5.3% YoY), and branded flour is holding across Texas distributors, so our best bet is to rebuild around the market that is already there: prioritize branded flour, build replacement volume through accounts still in play, improve low-to-mid protein pricing where it matters, reopen product gaps like tortilla flour, and stay close enough to step back in when Sysco wants a second source again. Because Houston and Dallas operator and distributor dynamics overlap, our AEs need to coordinate the two markets as one Texas plan, not two separate pushes.
Owners: Gus Patrick & Melissa Magsalin
Back the Southeast with the right network support
Atlanta, Tampa, Charlotte, Nashville
This is still the strongest region in the data, and the field keeps reinforcing it. Orlando already shows what closer support can do in Florida, and Atlanta is the next network unlock: with volume down 1.5% YoY on 23.8% share, the priority is understanding where those pounds moved, especially across pizza and manufacturing, and targeting cleaner-label, performance-led solutions there. A warehouse in Atlanta would ease freight across Atlanta, Charlotte, Nashville and more all at once, while making it easier to grow relationships like Gordon Food Service that are already showing momentum. In Tampa, where volume is up 13.9% YoY, the lever is distributor execution: activate distributors first, train them on the flour story, follow the highest-growth operators, and selectively target larger Hispanic bakeries, tourism, and pizza accounts.
Owners: Josh Cohen & Jeffrey Santiago
Build the Houston case with the right product and pricing
Houston
Houston is a 60.3 million pound market where we hold 20.4% share, less than half our national share, and the most ethnically mixed major metro in Texas with large-scale Hispanic, Asian, and African operator demand. Closing even part of that gap is worth roughly 9 million pounds a year from a single city. The barrier is not demand, it is mill proximity and pricing: Ardent mills in Houston, and large accounts like the Excel bakery (~44k CWT) come down to a BakeMark price we cannot match even at near-zero margin. Scope a focused plan with Gus Patrick aimed at the independent operator base: focus assortment on Hispanic and multicultural operators, pressure test pricing by operator type, and use product story and service to compete more effectively. The unlock is getting low-to-mid protein pricing right out of Arlington, which James and Gus are already working on.
Owner: Gus Patrick
Grow the West where it pays
Phoenix, Pacific Northwest, SoCal
The West is still pressured by freight, but the better opportunities are clearer now. Phoenix is not a blank-space market at an estimated ~35.6% share; it is an established base where the opportunity is validating which distributor-led lanes can scale, staying close to DSRs, and continuing to lean on Shamrock given its success in both Italian and Mexican markets. We also now have a presence with a new CAS and new AE in Phoenix. So, it remains the anchor while Las Vegas, Utah, and parts of the Mountain West are more underserved than the current map suggests, and Phoenix can double as a learning market for broader Southwest expansion. In the Pacific Northwest and Northern California, growth is more relationship-led than broad-based. In the Southwest more broadly, the bigger issue is having the right portfolio: cleaner-label, enzyme-based, untreated, and tortilla-oriented options that match where distributors like Shamrock see the market going.
Owners: Yoel Sharabi, Nancy Muller & Shotsie Wilson

What Comes Next

The first things to pick up

Start with a real cost-to-serve case for the Atlanta warehouse. It is the move with the widest reach and the one the field keeps returning to. From there: rebuild Texas around the market, a Houston growth plan with Gus Patrick, and a West Coast playbook built on the relationship-led approach Yoel and Nancy described. A recurring operational fix raised across territories is moving the direct minimum from three trucks to two, which would reopen a set of profitable, low-maintenance accounts.

One follow-up worth separating from the market work is product fit. Several AE conversations pointed to cases where the issue is not only freight or price, but whether the flour or bakery formula matches the application well enough to win. That came up in ethnic bread applications, Southeast soft wheat applications, and retail bakery shelf-life needs, and it is especially important in the West, where cleaner-label, untreated, unmalted, and enzyme-based needs do not always fit the current portfolio. The tortilla opportunity also deserves a closer look, especially in South-Central markets where several AEs pointed to Hispanic growth, cleaner-label expectations, and the need for a more competitive 10.7% to 11% protein flour option.

It is also starting to matter more in the Mid-Atlantic, where untreated, enzyme, and ascorbic-acid needs are growing alongside tighter distributor capacity. Some of the next opportunity may require product adjustment, not just better coverage, and bulk is worth a focused look given competitors have broader bulk networks than GMI’s bagged-flour strength.
