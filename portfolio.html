How the project came together

The Question

What was actually asked

Look at the top 50 metros and say where flour growth should come from.

Flour consumption overall is declining, and excess industry capacity makes growth harder. Even so, GMI flour volume is up 1.6% year over year, and for NAF to keep contributing, flour needs to deliver at least 1% growth annually. That meant finding the geographic pockets where GMI can grow differently, and more effectively, than the market around it.

The Approach

Four steps from data to answer

I sized the fifty largest metros against our mills and warehouses, read our own account-level volume and value, scored each market on five weighted lenses, and then put the ranking in front of the AEs who own these territories. Their context turned numbers into reasons and changed the framing in real ways. The final recommendation came out of both.

1
Sized the map. The fifty largest metros, pulled from Circana, Tableau, and census growth data, laid over our five mills and six warehouses.
2
Read our own book. Account-level volume and value by territory, distributor, and channel, in CWT, EQC & RNS.
3
Scored the markets. Five weighted lenses producing a ranked list, plus four stress tests to make sure the order held.
4
Called the field. Sat down with every single AE. Their context changed the read more than any single spreadsheet did.
The Big Picture

Why these are the True 5, not just the Top 5

A large market is not automatically a priority market. Take New York City: it is huge, but GMI already holds 57.5% flour share there, so extra focus would not create the same headroom as a market where we are less developed. That is the whole distinction between a Top 5 list and a True 5 list.

The True 5 are not simply the biggest metros or the highest raw scores. They are the five markets where size, share headroom, operator relevance, distributor access, and commercial practicality lined up the clearest, and where even a modest, one-point share gain creates meaningful upside across the group, though the size of that gain varies by metro, from roughly 1,994 CWT in Phoenix up to 6,032 CWT in Houston. Across the True 5 combined, a one-point gain represents an estimated 20,130 CWT and $563,650 in incremental RNS at $28/CWT.1

See the full scoring model, ranking, and stress tests in the case study.

1 CWT and RNS reflect each metro's own DMA-level total flour category volume (Circana Optika), divided by 100 to isolate one share point in pounds, then converted to CWT and priced at $28/CWT. Phoenix has no DMA-level total available and is back-calculated from GMI's own volume against its estimated regional share. Optika represents less than 50% of total market coverage, so these figures should be read as a conservative, directional floor.
What Connects the True 5

The common thread, and why it isn’t one playbook

These five metros are large flour markets with real share headroom, identifiable operator or distributor lanes, and practical next steps that make the opportunity actionable. Hispanic bakeries stand out across nearly all of them as a high-flour-use segment where GMI is still underpenetrated, and DSR accessibility keeps surfacing as the lever that turns opportunity into volume.

Even so, the path to growth is different in each market, so they should not be treated the same. The common logic is strong, but execution still has to match local economics, distributor structure, and operator mix — which is why the recommendation in the case study is four distinct moves, not one national push. Considerations that cut across all five include continuing to reevaluate product assortment, staying competitive on pricing strategy, and watching proximity to competition, especially where local mills sit close to our accounts.

What I Built

The deliverables

A deck for the panel, a one-page executive summary, an analysis workbook, an interactive map of all fifty metros, and this website. Each one tells the same story at a different level of detail, from a thirty-second read to the full build.

The map itself is built with Leaflet, Codex, VS Code, and GitHub. Mills are shown as diamonds, warehouses as squares, and metros as color-coded circles keyed to the legend. Clicking into a metro surfaces volume, companies in the area, cost-to-serve notes, and other metro-specific context, so the team can compare markets quickly and pressure test future decisions as new data comes in. The goal throughout was to leave behind a reusable handoff tool, not just a final presentation.

What I Learned

The part the data could not show

The best ideas did not all come from a dashboard. A day during sales immersion in Southern California spent with Nancy Muller and back to back days out in the field with James Mueting and Cathy Meyer showed how independent Hispanic bakeries buy, how a distributor route actually runs, and why a relationship can matter more than a price sheet. The macro data tells you which markets are big. The field tells you how to win them.
